**In brief**: The New York Times and other news outlets have filed lawsuits against the company that makes the privacy-focused Brave web browser, accusing it of scraping their content and helping users get around paywalls.

### Key points
- The New York Times is suing Brave, the maker of the privacy-focused browser, over alleged scraping of its copyrighted material.
- The core allegation is that the content was unlawfully distributed to Brave's own customers.
- TechCrunch names Anthropic and Perplexity as examples of those customers.
- The Verge, citing Reuters, says the suits also cover letting users bypass paywalls, in addition to scraping for AI training.
- The New York Daily News and the Chicago Tribune are among the publications named in connection with a complaint.

### What happened
TechCrunch reports that the New York Times has sued Brave, a company known for its privacy-focused browser. According to its account, the Times alleges Brave scraped its copyrighted work and then unlawfully distributed it to Brave's customers. TechCrunch lists Anthropic and Perplexity as examples of those customers.

The Verge covered the same event, drawing on Reuters reporting, and framed the dispute somewhat more broadly. It says the Times and a group of other publications filed lawsuits over alleged unlawful scraping to train AI, and also over letting users bypass their paywalls. It points to a complaint filed by the New York Daily News, the Chicago Tribune and other outlets. The source summary available to us is cut off, so the full list of plaintiffs and the detailed claims are not visible here.

The two outlets emphasize different angles. TechCrunch focuses on distribution: content allegedly scraped and then supplied to AI companies. The Verge highlights two separate theories, AI training and paywall circumvention. These read as complementary descriptions of the same litigation rather than conflicting accounts.

### Why it matters
The suits are notable for targeting a browser maker rather than an AI developer directly. If the claims hold, they could raise the question of whether companies sitting between publishers and AI services can be held responsible for how content is collected and passed along. Brave has built its identity around privacy, so the allegations also touch on how the company is perceived.

The paywall allegation adds another dimension. Beyond the copying of articles for AI use, it reflects publishers' concern that subscription models can be undermined. The sources do not describe Brave's response or any court timeline, so those remain open points to watch as the cases proceed.
